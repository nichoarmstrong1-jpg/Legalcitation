## White Pages (Full Academic Rules)

### Rule 11: Constitutions
[THE DIAGRAM — Annotated Citation to a U.S. Constitutional Provision
Above the diagram is the introductory line: "Citation of Section 2 of the Fourteenth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution:"
The diagram shows the following citation in large, bold text:
U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 2.
Arrows and labels point to each piece of the citation. Reading from left to right:
"U.S. Const." is labeled underneath as the "abbreviation of constitution cited." This tells you the source is the United States Constitution. The jurisdiction abbreviation "U.S." comes first, followed by "Const." which is the standard abbreviation for "Constitution."
Then "amend." is labeled above as the "abbreviation for 'amendment.'" This is telling you that the subdivision being cited is an amendment, and "amend." is how you abbreviate that word in a citation.
Then "XIV," is labeled above as the "no. of amendment cited." This is the Roman numeral fourteen, identifying that you are citing the Fourteenth Amendment specifically.
Then "§ 2." is labeled underneath as the "section symbol and no. of section cited." The section symbol (§) followed by the number 2 tells the reader you are pointing to Section 2 within that amendment.
So reading the whole citation together, it says: United States Constitution, Amendment Fourteen, Section 2. The diagram breaks it apart to show you that every constitutional citation follows this pattern — jurisdiction abbreviation, then "Const.," then the type of subdivision (like "amend." or "art."), then the number of that subdivision, then any further sub-subdivision like a section number.
THE CHART — Citation and Text Forms for Different Types of Constitutions
Near the bottom of the page, there is a chart with three columns and three rows of content plus a header row. The three column headers are, from left to right: (1) an unlabeled category column on the far left, (2) "Citation," and (3) "Text." Each row shows a different type of constitution and demonstrates what the formal citation looks like versus how you would refer to it when writing in running text.
Row 1 — U.S. Constitution
The far left column identifies this row as "U.S. Constitution." The Citation column shows: U.S. Const. art. IV, § 1. The Text column shows two options: "Article IV, Section 1" or "the Full Faith and Credit Clause."
What this row is telling you is that when formally citing a provision of the U.S. Constitution, you use the abbreviated form with "U.S. Const." followed by the article and section numbers. But when you are writing in running text, you either spell out the article and section numbers in full (capitalizing "Article" and "Section") or you may refer to the provision by its commonly known name — in this case, the Full Faith and Credit Clause.
Row 2 — State Constitution
The far left column identifies this row as "State constitution." The Citation column shows: S.C. Const. art. I, § 12. The Text column shows two options: "article I, section 12" or "the double jeopardy clause."
What this row is telling you is that for a state constitution, the citation form follows the same pattern — the state abbreviation, then "Const.," then the article and section. In running text, you spell out the article and section (but notice that for state constitutions, "article" and "section" are lowercase, unlike the U.S. Constitution where they are capitalized). Alternatively, you can refer to the provision by its common name, here "the double jeopardy clause."
Row 3 — Nongovernmental Organization Constitution
The far left column identifies this row as "Nongovernmental organization constitution." The Citation column shows: World Health Org. Const. ch. 2, art II, § (a). The Text column shows two options: "chapter 2, article II, section (a)" or "coordination clause."
What this row is telling you is that constitutions of nongovernmental organizations follow the same general pattern — the organization name abbreviated, then "Const.," then the subdivisions of that particular constitution (here using "ch." for chapter, "art" for article, and "§" for section). In running text, you spell out the subdivision names in lowercase, or you can refer to the provision by a descriptive name like "coordination clause."Generally, cite constitutions by the abbreviated name of the jurisdiction (as indicated in table T10) and the word “Const.” Constitutions of nongovernmental entities should also be abbreviated according to table T6. Abbreviate the subdivisions of constitutions, such as articles and clauses, according to table T16:

Follow the numbering convention of each individual constitution:

But also: La. Const. art. X, pt. IV.


Cite constitutional provisions currently in force without a date. If the cited provision has been repealed, either indicate parenthetically the fact and year of repeal or cite the repealing provision in full:



U.S. Const. amend. XVIII (repealed 1933).


U.S. Const. amend. XVIII, repealed by, U.S. Const. amend. XXI.


When citing a provision that has been subsequently amended, either indicate parenthetically the fact and year of amendment or cite the amending provision in full:



U.S. Const. art. I, § 3, cl. 1 (amended 1913).


U.S. Const. art. I, § 3, cl. 1, amended by, U.S. Const. amend. XVII.


Cite constitutions that have been totally superseded or are otherwise no longer in effect by year of adoption; if the specific provision cited was adopted in a different year than the constitution, give that year parenthetically:



Articles of Confederation of 1781, art. IX, para. 1.


Ark. Const. of 1868, art. III, § 2 (1873).


When citing a constitution contained in an electronic database, indicate parenthetically the name of the publisher, editor, or compiler unless the constitution is published, edited, or compiled by or under the supervision of, federal or state officials. Also indicate the name of the database and information regarding the currentness of the database as provided by the database itself:



Wash. Const. art. I, § 2 (West, Westlaw through Nov. 2024 amendments).


P.R. Const. art. II, § 1 (LexisNexis, LEXIS through June 2024).


When referring to multiple amendments, sections within the same article, or clauses within the same section, it is permissible to use a single citation clause. Otherwise, the second citation should use "id." rather than repeat the name of the constitution:



U.S. Const. amends. V, XIV.


U.S. Const. art. I, § 8; id. art. II, § 2.


Do not use a short citation form (other than “id.”) for constitutions.
