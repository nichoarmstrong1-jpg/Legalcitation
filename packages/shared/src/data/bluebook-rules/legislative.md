# Legislative Materials — Bluebook B13 + Rule 13

## Bluepages (Practitioner Rules)
### B13 Legislative Materials
[B13 Legislative Materials (pp. 22–23)
A full citation to legislative material includes the following components, though not necessarily in the following order: (1) the title of the material; (2) the abbreviated name of the legislative body; (3) the number assigned to the material; (4) the number of the Congress and/or legislative session; and (5) the year of publication.



H.R. Rep. No. 99-253, pt. 1, at 54 (1985).


S. Rep. No. 84-2, at 7 (1955).


Enacted Federal Bill



When citing federal bills, include the name of the bill (if relevant), the abbreviated name of the Congressional chamber, the number of the bill, the number of the Congress, the section (if any), and the year of publication:



S. 1983, 93rd Cong. § 10 (1973).


H.R. 8638, 95th Cong. § 2 (1977).


Freedom of Information Act, S. 1160, 88th Cong. § 1 (1965).


Digital Millennium Copyright Act, H.R. 2281, 105th Cong. § 6 (1997).


Unenacted Federal Bill



Protection from Personal Intrusion Act, H.R. 2448, 105th Cong. § 2(a) (1997).


Federal Congressional Hearing



Toxic Substances Control Act: Hearings on S. 776 Before the Subcomm. on the Env’t of the S. Comm. on Com., 94th Cong. 343 (1975).


Federal Congressional Report



H.R. Rep. No. 99-253, pt. 1, at 54 (1985).


S. Rep. No. 84-2, at 7 (1955).


State Bills and Resolutions



When citing state bills and resolutions, include: (1) the name of the legislative body; (2) the number of the bill or resolution; (3) the number of the legislative body; and (4) the number of the legislative session. Parenthetically indicate the name of the state and the year of enactment or publication:



H.B. 636, 1999 Gen. Assemb., 413th Sess. (Md. 1999).


H.R. 189, 145th Gen. Assemb., Reg. Sess. (Ga. 1999).


For further guidance on citing legislative materials, see rule 13.]

## White Pages (Full Academic Rules)
### Rule 13: Legislative Materials
[13 Legislative Materials (p.140)
Besides statutes (rule 12), the legislative process generates bills and resolutions (rule 13.2); committee hearings (rule 13.3); reports, documents, and committee prints (rule 13.4); floor debates (rule 13.5); and, sometimes, separately bound legislative histories (rule 13.6). When citing any United States legislative material except debates, include the title (if relevant), the abbreviated name of the house, the number of the Congress, the number assigned to the material, and the year of publication. State legislative materials are cited similarly except when indicated otherwise. Abbreviations for commonly used words in legislative materials are listed in table T9.



In addition, include parenthetically the session number for House and Senate documents published before the 60th Congress (1907), House Reports published before the 47th Congress (1881), and Senate Reports published before the 40th Congress (1867). For House and Senate materials published after these dates, the session number can be inferred from the year of publication: First sessions always fall in odd-numbered years, while second sessions always fall in even-numbered years. On rare occasions, Congress holds a third session. When citing materials produced during a third session, provide this information parenthetically.]

**What it is:** This page is Bluebook Rule 13.1 — "Basic Citation Forms" for Legislative Materials. It contains a **reference table** that acts as a quick-look guide, showing one correctly formatted example citation for each of the major types of legislative materials you might need to cite. The table has **2 columns** and **11 rows** (one per source type, with "Federal bill" getting two example rows).

---

**The 2 columns explained:**

1. **Left column (Type of legislative material)** — Names the category of legislative source.
2. **Right column (Example citation)** — Shows a properly formatted Bluebook citation for that category.

---

**The 11 rows explained, one by one:**

**Row 1 — Federal bill (unenacted), example 1:**
- "Privacy Protection Act of 1998, H.R. 3224, 105th Cong. § 2(a) (1998)."
- This shows how to cite a federal bill that was introduced but never became law. It includes the bill's title, its House of Representatives bill number (H.R. 3224), which Congress it was introduced in (105th), the specific section cited (§ 2(a)), and the year.

**Row 2 — Federal bill (unenacted), example 2:**
- "H.R. 119, 54th Cong. (1st Sess. 1896)."
- A second example for an older bill. Because it's from before 1907, the session number (1st Sess.) must be included parenthetically — something not needed for modern bills where the session can be inferred from the year.

**Row 3 — Federal resolution (unenacted):**
- "H.R.J. Res. 79, 106th Cong. (1999)."
- Shows how to cite a joint resolution from the House. The format is similar to a bill but uses the resolution abbreviation (H.R.J. Res.) instead of a bill number.

**Row 4 — State bill:**
- "H.R. 124, 179th Leg., 1st Spec. Sess. (Pa. 1995)."
- Shows how to cite a state-level bill. Instead of a Congress number, it uses the legislature number (179th Leg.) and the session designation (1st Spec. Sess.), plus the state abbreviation (Pa.) in the date parenthetical.

**Row 5 — State resolution:**
- "S.J. Res. 836, 118th Leg., 3d Spec. Sess. (Me. 1999)."
- Same pattern as a state bill, but for a state senate joint resolution. Uses the state abbreviation (Me. for Maine) and the legislature/session information.

**Row 6 — Committee hearing:**
- "Background and History of Impeachment: Hearing Before the Subcomm. on the Constitution of the H. Comm. on the Judiciary, 105th Cong. 22–23 (1998) (statement of Rep. Asa Hutchinson, Member, H. Comm. on the Judiciary)."
- The most detailed example. It includes the hearing title, the subcommittee and committee name, the Congress number, the specific pages cited (22–23), the year, and a parenthetical identifying whose statement is being cited.

**Row 7 — Federal report:**
- "H.R. Rep. No. 101-524, at 10 (1990), reprinted in 1990 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1448, 1451."
- Shows a House report citation with its report number (101-524, where 101 is the Congress number), a pinpoint page (at 10), the year, and a cross-reference to where it was reprinted in the United States Code Congressional and Administrative News (U.S.C.C.A.N.).

**Row 8 — Federal document:**
- "H.R. Doc. No. 102-399, at 3 (1992)."
- Similar to a report but for a House document. Simpler format with the document number, pinpoint page, and year.

**Row 9 — Committee print:**
- "Staff of H. Comm. on the Judiciary, 93d Cong., Constitutional Grounds for Presidential Impeachment 38 (Comm. Print 1974)."
- Committee prints are cited as works by an institutional author (the committee staff). Includes the committee name, Congress number, the title of the print, the page number (38), and the parenthetical identifying it as a committee print with the year.

**Row 10 — Congressional debate:**
- "145 Cong. Rec. H1817 (daily ed. Apr. 12, 1999) (statement of Rep. Edward Pease)."
- Shows how to cite the Congressional Record. Includes the volume (145), the abbreviation "Cong. Rec.", the page number with chamber prefix (H1817 for House pages), the date of the daily edition, and a parenthetical identifying the speaker.

**Row 11 — Source reprinted in separately bound legislative history:**
- "S. Comm. on Lab. and Pub. Welfare, Labor-Management Reporting and Disclosure Act of 1959, S. Rep. No. 86-187, at 4 (1959), reprinted in 1959 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2318, 2320, and in 1 NLRB, Legislative History of the Labor-Management Reporting and Disclosure Act of 1959, at 397, 400 (1959)."
- The most complex example. It's a Senate report that has been reprinted in both U.S.C.C.A.N. and in a separately bound legislative history compiled by the NLRB. The citation chains together the original source and both reprint locations.

Before you dive into the detailed sub-rules (13.2 through 13.8, each covering one category in depth), this table gives you a model citation for every major type of legislative material — from bills and resolutions, to hearings, reports, documents, committee prints, floor debates, and reprinted legislative histories. #### 13.2 Bills and Resolutions
[13.2 Bills and Resolutions (pp. 141–42)
(a) Unenacted federal bills and resolutions.
 When citing federal bills, include the name of the bill (if relevant), the abbreviated name of the house, the number of the bill, the number of the Congress, the section (if any), and the year of publication:
S. 516, 105th Cong. § 2 (1997).


H.R. 422, 106th Cong. (1999).


Clear Skies Act, S. 485, 108th Cong. (2003).


Protection from Personal Intrusion Act, H.R. 2448, 105th Cong. § 2(a) (1997).


A parenthetical indicating the date and stage of the bill may be provided in order to distinguish among multiple versions of the same bill in the same Congress. Subcommittee and committee names may be abbreviated according to tables T6, T9, and T10:



S. 593, 101st Cong. § 2 (as passed by Senate, May 31, 1989).


S. 593, 101st Cong. § 2 (as reported by S. Comm. on the Judiciary, May 12, 1989).


Cite resolutions analogously, using the following abbreviations:



House Resolution	H.R. Res.
Senate Resolution	S. Res.
House Concurrent Resolution	H.R. Con. Res.
Senate Concurrent Resolution	S. Con. Res.
House Joint Resolution	H.R.J. Res.
Senate Joint Resolution	S.J. Res.
Senate Executive Resolution	S. Exec. Res.

Thus:



H.R.J. Res. 124, 105th Cong. (1998).


A parallel citation to a published committee hearing, a legislative report, or the Congressional Record may also be provided if it would assist the reader in locating the bill (rules 13.3, 13.4, and 13.5).



(b) Enacted federal bills and resolutions.
 Enacted bills and joint resolutions are statutes (rule 12). They are cited as statutes except when used to document legislative history, in which case they are cited as unenacted bills.
Enacted simple resolutions, which bind only one house of Congress, and enacted concurrent resolutions, which bind either one or both houses of Congress, should be cited as unenacted bills. Unless otherwise clear in context, the fact of enactment should be noted parenthetically:



S. Res. 141, 106th Cong. (1999) (enacted).


H.R. Con. Res. 196, 106th Cong. (1999) (enacted).


H.R. Res. 811, 108th Cong. (2004) (enacted).


A parallel citation to the Congressional Record (for simple resolutions) or to the Statutes at Large (for concurrent resolutions) may be provided if it would assist the reader in locating the enacted resolution. Because only enacted resolutions are printed in the Statutes at Large (“Stat.”), a parenthetical noting enactment is not necessary when a parallel citation to Stat. is given:



S. Res. 218, 83d Cong., 100 Cong. Rec. 2972 (1954) (enacted).


S. Con. Res. 97, 94th Cong., 90 Stat. 3024 (1976).


(c) State bills and resolutions.
 When citing state bills and resolutions, include the name of the legislative body, abbreviated according to tables T6, T9, and T10, the number of the bill or resolution, the number of the legislative body (or, if not numbered, the year of the body), and the number or designation of the legislative session. Parenthetically indicate the name of the state, abbreviated according to table T10, and the year of enactment (for an enacted bill or resolution) or the year of publication (for an unenacted bill or resolution):
H.B. 636, 1999 Gen. Assemb., 413th Sess. (Md. 1999).


H.R. 189, 145th Gen. Assemb., Reg. Sess. (Ga. 1999).


A parallel citation to state session laws may be provided if it would assist the reader in locating an enacted resolution:



H.R.J. Res. 1, 40th Leg., 2d Spec. Sess., 1974 Utah Laws 7.]


####  13.3 Hearings
[13.3 Hearings (pp. 142–43)
(a) Federal committee hearings.
 When citing federal committee hearings, always include the entire subject matter title as it appears on the cover, the bill number (if any), the subcommittee name (if any), the committee name, the number of the Congress, the page number of the particular material being cited (if any), and the year of publication. Subcommittee and committee names may be abbreviated according to tables T6, T9, and T10. Cite as follows:
Protection from Personal Intrusion Act and Privacy Protection Act of 1998: Hearing on H.R. 2448 and H.R. 3224 Before the H. Comm. on the Judiciary, 105th Cong. 56–57 (1998) (statement of Richard Masur, President, Screen Actors Guild).


Copyright Protection for Semiconductor Chips: Hearing on H.R. 1028 Before the Subcomm. on Cts., C.L. & the Admin. of Just. of the H. Comm. on the Judiciary, 98th Cong. 14 (1983) (statement of Jon A. Baumgarten, Copyright Counsel, Ass’n of Am. Publishers).


Titles of the individuals providing statements may be abbreviated according to tables T6, T9, and T11:



Tribal Energy Self-Sufficiency Act and the Native American Energy Development and Self-Determination Act: Hearing on S. 424 and S. 522 Before the S. Comm. on Indian Affs., 108th Cong. 1 (2003) (statement of Sen. Ben Nighthorse Campbell, Chairman, S. Comm. on Indian Affs.).


Transforming the Federal Government to Protect America from Terrorism: Hearing Before the H. Select Comm. on Homeland Sec., 107th Cong. 23–25 (2002) (statement of John Ashcroft, Att’y Gen. of the U.S.).


(b) State committee hearings.
 When citing state committee hearings, follow the same form, but also include the number of the legislative session:
Tax Credit for Cost of Providing Commuter Benefits to Employees: Hearing on H.D. 636 Before the H. Comm. on Ways & Means, 1999 Leg., 413th Sess. 5–8 (Md. 1999) (statement of Del. Paul Carlson, Member, H. Comm. on Ways & Means).]

####  13.4 Reports, Documents, and Committee Prints
[13.4 Reports, Documents, and Committee Prints (pp. 143–45)
(a) Numbered federal reports and documents.
 Citations to numbered federal reports should include the name of the house, the number of the Congress connected by a hyphen to the number of the report, the part and/or page number on which material being cited appears, and the year of publication. Use small capitals for the house, abbreviation of report, and abbreviation of number:
H.R. Rep. No. 99-253, pt. 1, at 54 (1985).


S. Rep. No. 84-2, at 7 (1955).


Cite conference reports using the following format:



H.R. Rep. No. 98-1037, at 3 (1984) (Conf. Rep.).


S. Rep. No. 95-601, at 5 (1977) (Conf. Rep.).


Cite numbered federal documents analogously, using the following abbreviations:



House Document	H.R. Doc. No.
Senate Document	S. Doc. No.
House Miscellaneous Document	H.R. Misc. Doc. No.

The following citation formats are often used to designate international agreements to which the United States is a party (rule 21.4.5(a)):



Senate Executive Document	S. Exec. Doc. No.
Senate Treaty Document	S. Treaty Doc. No.

When possible (and particularly for documents published after 1974), give a parallel citation to the permanent edition of United States Code Congressional and Administrative News (rule 12.6):



S. Rep. No. 95-797, at 4 (1978), as reprinted in 1978 U.S.C.C.A.N. 9260, 9263.


(b) Titles and authors.
 Titles of numbered reports or documents may be indicated; if the title is given, the author should also be named (see rule 15.1(c) regarding institutional authors):
Carlton Koepge, The Road to Industrial Peace, H.R. Doc. No. 82-563, at 29–30 (1953).


U.S. Immigr. Comm’n, Immigration Legislation, S. Doc. No. 61-758, at 613 (3d Sess. 1911).


(c) Unnumbered federal documents and committee prints.
 Committee prints and unnumbered documents must be cited as works of institutional authors (rule 15.1(c)). Note that the number of the Congress is part of the author’s name:
Staff of S. Comm. on the Judiciary, 81st Cong., Report on Antitrust Law 17 (Comm. Print 1950).


If the document or committee print is primarily the work of specific persons, that fact may be noted parenthetically.



(d) Federal legislative agency reports.
 Legislative reports, like those of the Congressional Research Service (CRS) and the Government Accountability Office (GAO), must be cited as works of institutional authors (rule 15.1(c)); include the report number (as given by the agency) as part of the title:
Louis Fisher, Cong. Rsch. Serv., RL31340, Military Tribunals: The Quirin Precedent 30 (2002).


U.S. Gov't Accountability Off., GAO-08-751, Food and Drug Administration: Approval and Oversight of the Drug Mifeprex 27 (2008).


(e) Federal legislative journals.
 Cite congressional journals according to the following model:
S. Journal, 24th Cong., 2d Sess. 123–24 (1836).


(f) State materials.
 Citations to state legislative reports, documents, and similar materials should include the title of the document and its author if available (see rule 15.1(c)), the name of the legislative body abbreviated according to tables T6, T9, and T10, the number of the legislative body or year, depending on the state's practice, connected by a hyphen to the number of the report or document if available, the number of the legislative session, the part or page number on which the material being cited appears, and the year of publication. Unless it is clear from the title or author information appearing in the citation, provide the name of the state abbreviated according to table T10 parenthetically:
Commonwealth of Pa. Dep't of Agric., Annual Report of the State Food Purchase Program, S. 178-247, 1st Sess., at 4 (1994).]

####  13.5 Debates
[13.5 Debates (p.140)
Cite congressional debates after 1873 to the Congressional Record; use the daily edition only for matters not yet appearing in the permanent edition. The primary ways in which the Congressional Record differs from the daily edition are continuous pagination, altered text, and the dropping of the prefixes H, S, and E before page numbers:



123 Cong. Rec. 17147 (1977).


131 Cong. Rec. S11465–66 (daily ed. Sep. 13, 1985) (statement of Sen. Malcolm Wallop).


Cite congressional debates through 1873 according to the following models:



1837–1873	Cong. Globe, 36th Cong., 1st Sess. 1672 (1860).
1824–1837	10 Reg. Deb. 3472 (1834).
1789–1824	38 Annals of Cong. 624 (1822).

If the debate occurred in a different year from its publication in the Congressional Record, cite the year in which the debate was published.



For volume one of the Annals give the name(s) of the editor(s) and year of publication in parentheses:



1 Annals of Cong. 486 (1789) (Joseph Gales ed., 1834).]

#### 13.6 Separately Bound Legislative Histories
[13.6 Separately Bound Legislative Histories (p.145)
The legislative histories of several important acts are published separately (e.g., the Administrative Procedure Act, Titles VII and IX of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1970, the Equal Employment Opportunity Act, the Internal Revenue Acts, the National Labor Relations Act, the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970, and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934). If it would aid the reader in locating the source, a parallel citation may be given to such a separate publication. Cite these publications according to rule 15 (books, reports, and other nonperiodic materials):



H.R. Rep. No. 80-245, at 6 (1947), reprinted in 1 NLRB, Legislative History of the Labor-Management Relations Act, 1947, at 292, 297 (1948).



Internal Revenue Amendments, Pub. L. No. 87-834, § 15(a), 76 Stat. 960, 1041–42 (1962) (codified at I.R.C. § 1248(a)), reprinted in Joint Comm. on Internal Revenue Tax’n, 90th Cong., Legislative History of the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, at 473–74 (1967).]

#### 13.7Electronic Media and Online Sources
[13.7 Electronic Media and Online Sources (p.146)
(a) Commercial electronic databases.
 When citing to materials contained in a commercial electronic database, give the name of the database and any identifying codes or numbers that uniquely identify the material. If the name of the database is not clear from the database identifier, include it parenthetically at the end of the citation:
Federal bill (unenacted)	H.R. 3781, 104th Cong. § 2(b) (1996), 1996 CONG US HR 3781 (Westlaw).
Federal report	H.R. Rep. No. 92-98 (1971), reprinted in 1971 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1017, 1971 WL 11312.
Congressional debate	142 Cong. Rec. H11,460 (daily ed. Sep. 27, 1996) (statement of Rep. John Tanner), 142 Cong Rec H 11452, at *H11,460 (LEXIS).

(b) Internet and online sources.
 Where a print version is accessible, citation should be made to the print source. Where, however, the print source is difficult or impossible to obtain or the governing authority has designated the electronic source as the official version, citation should be made to the electronic source with consideration of the general principles in rule 18 regarding authentication and preferred document formats:
An Act Prohibiting Writing a Text Message While Driving, H.R. 34, 2009 Sess. (N.H. 2009), http://www.gencourt.state.nh.us/legislation/2009/HB0034.html (on file with author).]

#### 13.8 Short Forms for Legislative Materials]
[Here's a plain-text explanation of the chart and other visual elements on this page:

**What it is:** This page is Bluebook Rule 13.8 — "Short Forms for Legislative Materials." It contains a **reference table** showing how to abbreviate citations for legislative materials depending on whether you're writing in prose or in a citation. There are also "before and after" examples for electronic sources below the table. The table has **4 columns** and **4 rows** of examples (plus a header row).

---

**The surrounding text (sections a–c) that set up the table:**

**(a) Main text** — When you mention a legislative material in the body of your law review article, use the "Text" column form and put a full or short citation in a footnote.

**(b) Footnote text** — Same rule applies when mentioning legislative materials within footnote prose: use the "Text" column form, and add a citation clause or sentence.

**(c) Citations** — When writing an actual citation in a footnote, use the "Short Citation" column form — but only if the material has already been fully cited in the same footnote or can be readily found (including via "id.") in one of the five preceding footnotes. If not, use the "Full Citation" form. The text also notes you can check Rule 4.2(a) for guidance on using "supra."

---

**The 4 columns explained:**

1. **(Left-most / Source type)** — Identifies the category of legislative material.
2. **"Full Citation"** — The complete, first-time citation with all identifying details.
3. **"Text"** — The plain-language form you use when referring to the material in the body of your writing (not in a citation).
4. **"Short Citation"** — The abbreviated citation form you can use after the full citation has already appeared nearby.

---

**The 4 rows (examples) explained:**

**Row 1 — Federal Bill (unenacted):**
- Full citation: "H.R. 3055, 94th Cong. (1976)" — includes the bill number, the Congress number, and the year.
- Text form: "House Bill 3055" — spelled out in plain English for prose. No abbreviations, no Congress number.
- Short citation: "H.R. 3055" — just the bill designation and number, dropping the Congress and year.

**Row 2 — State Resolution:**
- Full citation: "S. Res. 20, 37th Leg., 2d Sess. (Okla. 1979)" — includes the resolution number, the legislature number, the session, the state abbreviation, and year.
- Text form: "Oklahoma Senate Resolution 20" — fully spelled out with the state name for prose.
- Short citation: "Okla. S. Res. 20" — abbreviated but still includes the state abbreviation to keep it identifiable.

**Row 3 — Federal Report:**
- Full citation: "H.R. Rep. No. 92-98 (1971)" — includes the report designation, the report number (where 92 is the Congress number and 98 is the report number), and the year.
- Text form: "House Report 98" — spelled out plainly, using only the report number (dropping the Congress number prefix).
- Short citation: "H.R. Rep. No. 92-98" — same as the full citation but drops the year parenthetical.

**Row 4 — Federal Document:**
- Full citation: "H.R. Doc. No. 94-208 (1975)" — same pattern as reports, with the document number (94 = Congress, 208 = document number) and year.
- Text form: "House Document 208" — spelled out, using only the document number.
- Short citation: "H.R. Doc. No. 94-208" — drops the year parenthetical.

---

**Below the table — section (d) Electronic sources:**

This section shows a "before and after" example for materials found in electronic databases like Westlaw:

- Full citation: "H.R. 3781, 104th Cong. § 1 (1996), 1996 Cong US HR 3781 (Westlaw)." — includes everything plus the Westlaw database identifier.
- Short form becomes: "H.R. 3781 § 1, 1996 Cong US HR 3781 (Westlaw)." — drops the Congress number and year but keeps the database identifier so the reader can locate it electronically.

The page also notes that for materials available only online, you use the normal short form and don't need to repeat the URL after the first full citation.

---

**In short,** this table follows the same pattern as the short-form tables for statutes (Rule 12.10) and regulations (Rule 14.6). It's a quick-reference showing three levels of detail for four types of legislative materials: the full formal citation for first use, a plain-English version for prose, and a trimmed-down citation for subsequent references. The key pattern across all four rows is that the "Text" form spells things out in readable English (e.g., "House Bill," "House Report"), while the "Short Citation" form keeps the abbreviations but drops the year and other details that are no longer needed after the full cite has appeared.]
